# 🇯🇵 Organized Crime & Land Assembly: Japan's Real Estate Bubble History

This document explores the historical and sociological accuracy of the real estate and loan-sharking setting in **"G弦上的魔王" (The Devil on G-String)**. In the story, the conflict surrounding the redevelopment of Tsubaki’s hometown and the division of labor between "clean" developers like *Sanno Property* and yakuza-style enforcers like *Azai Gonzou's organization* is deeply rooted in the social history of Japan's **Asset Price Bubble Era (late 1980s – early 1990s)**.

---

## 1. The Phenomenon of *Jiage* (地上げ) and *Jiageya* (地上げ屋)

In Japanese real estate, **Jiage** (地上げ, literally "land-raising" or "land-assembly") is the process of buying up contiguous small plots of land owned by different individuals to consolidate them into a single large property suitable for major redevelopment (e.g., shopping malls, hotels, or resort facilities, like the amusement park/skating rink in the game).

During the bubble economy of the late 1980s, real estate prices skyrocketed, making land assembly incredibly lucrative. However, it was also highly difficult because:
* Urban Japanese land is extremely fragmented.
* Mainstream corporations did not want to be seen harassing or strong-arming elderly tenants or homeowners who refused to move.

To bypass this, developers and major banks hired third-party contractors known as **Jiageya** (地上げ屋, land assemblers/evictors) to clear the properties. The *jiageya* were almost always yakuza front companies (*kigyō shatei*) or underworld enforcers.

---

## 2. Why the Yakuza? The Tenant Protection Act (*Shakuchi Shakka Hō*)

In the West, developers can sometimes rely on government eminent domain, or landlords can evict tenants once a lease expires. In Japan, however, the **Landlord and Tenant Act (借地借家法 - *Shakuchi Shakka Hō*)** grants tenants and small landholders extraordinary legal protections:
* **Automatic Renewal**: A lease is automatically renewed unless the landlord can prove a "justifiable reason" (*seito jiyū*), which courts historically interpreted very strictly (commercial redevelopment was rarely accepted as a valid reason).
* **Eviction Protections**: Even if a landlord wants to demolish a building, they cannot legally evict tenants without paying massive buyout sums (*tachinokiryō*).

Because legal eviction was practically impossible, *jiageya* used extra-legal coercion to force residents to sell or leave "voluntarily." 

### Underworld Coercion Tactics
* **Intimidation**: Constant threatening phone calls, dumping trash on lawns, or parking large commercial vehicles to block sunlight/access.
* **Buying Surrounding Plots**: Purchasing the properties surrounding a holdout family, then demolishing them or renting them to loud, disruptive tenants to make the area unlivable.
* **Predatory Debt Squeezing**: Buying up the mortgages or local debts of the landowners, then demanding immediate repayment or seizing their land rights in lieu of cash. This is the exact business model of **Azai Gonzou (浅井权三)** in *G-senjou no Maou*.

---

## 3. The Banking & Corporate Infiltration

A major revelation of the post-bubble "Lost Decades" was the extent to which respectable, blue-chip Japanese corporations and banks funded organized crime:
* **The Money Trail**: Mainstream banks, unable to lend directly to yakuza, would funnel billions of yen through intermediate leasing and finance companies (*jusen*) to yakuza-controlled real estate front companies.
* **Bad Loans**: When the bubble burst in 1991, land prices collapsed. The massive loans extended to yakuza *jiageya* could not be repaid. This led to Japan's massive **Bad Loan Crisis**, which crippled the Japanese banking system for over a decade.

Kyousuke’s line in scenario `g07`:
> *"Sanno Property is a clean company, so the dirty work was thrown to us."*

perfectly reflects how elite Japanese corporations maintained plausible deniability while knowingly utilizing yakuza organizations to execute their business strategies.

## 📖 Recommended References & Scholarly Reading

For those interested in studying this era of Japanese economic and organized crime history, the following sources are highly recommended:

### 1. Academic Monographs
* **Hill, Peter B. E. (2003). *The Japanese Mafia: Yakuza, Law, and the State*. Oxford University Press.**
  > [!IMPORTANT]
  > This is the definitive academic work on the yakuza's transition from traditional street gangs to modern corporate rackets, detailing how the bubble economy transformed organized crime into corporate finance and real estate players.
* **Syme, Anthony. (2002). *The Bank of Japan and the Japanese Economy*. Routledge.**
  > Explains the financial and regulatory failures that allowed yakuza-tied real estate speculation to trigger the banking collapse.

### 2. Investigative Journalism & Memoirs
* **Adelstein, Jake. (2009). *Tokyo Vice: An American Reporter on the Police Beat in Japan*. Pantheon.**
  > Offers a first-hand account of yakuza corporate fronts and the intersection between finance, journalism, and organized crime.
* **Kaplan, David E., & Dubro, Alec. (2003). *Yakuza: Japan's Criminal Underworld*. University of California Press.**
  > A comprehensive historical overview of the yakuza's involvement in post-war reconstruction, politics, and the bubble-era financial scandals.

### 3. Cultural Portrayals
* **Sega Games. (2015). *Yakuza 0* (龍が如く0 誓いの场所).**
  > A video game that serves as a highly accurate historical recreation of 1980s bubble-era Tokyo and Osaka, focusing heavily on the frantic *jiageya* real estate battles over the "Empty Lot."
